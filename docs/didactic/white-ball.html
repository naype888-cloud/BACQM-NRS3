The ball of yarn — why a star that does not collapse shines on its face

Everything you see is a field that has been excited and is being measured; the photon is the carrier that brings it to your eye. Where there is no excitation there is nothing to measure: the black of "nothing there" is only a possibility. A star whose core stays below its collapse mass spreads its matter over many quanta and fills none of them. It wraps itself in layers and layers of quanta, stacked on the threads along which the hopping moves the excitations, like a football with a LED on every vertex and clear panels in between. From outside you only see the lit face; the layers behind it stay intact. If too much falls into the same quantum, that quantum fills, collapses, and no light returns.

The ball: threads, layers and the lit face

Dots: the hopping state |Ψ|² on every position. Clear walls: the layers. Front wall: the face, one LED per step. Drag to turn.

The core mass decides the colour

Below 2.08 M☉ the face shines; from 3.2 M☉ the quantum is full and dark. In between, the measured stars do not decide where the collapse mass is.

What is exact here

  • Lean Every face of the outer wall hides the same number of interiors, 2^(layers·dy·dz): from outside you cannot tell what is inside.
  • Lean Spread over the quanta with none full, every layer keeps light; one full layer is one dark quantum.
  • Lean The Chandrasekhar mass from CODATA 2018 and IAU 2015 lies between 1.43 and 1.44 M☉.
  • Declared bridge A quantum below capacity shines on its edge, a full one is dark; the core fills its quantum at the collapse mass, somewhere between 2.08 and 3.2 M☉.
  • Browser The brightness of each LED is |Ψ|² at the two ends of its step; the drawing and the colours are the page's, not theorems.
